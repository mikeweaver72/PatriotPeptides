How to Evaluate Sample Integrity Before Research

2026-09-23 · All research notes

A peptide sample can arrive with a stated purity value and a clean-looking vial, yet still be unsuitable for a specific research workflow. Integrity is not one data point. It is the documented condition of a material from release through receipt, storage, preparation, and analysis. Knowing how to evaluate sample integrity gives qualified researchers a disciplined way to decide whether a sample should enter a study, be held for review, or be excluded.

For Research Use Only materials, that decision should begin before the vial is opened. A defensible evaluation connects supplier documentation to receiving records and the actual demands of the planned analytical work.

What Sample Integrity Means in a Research Setting

Sample integrity describes whether a material remains representative of its documented identity, composition, and expected condition. For peptide research materials, the assessment typically includes identity, purity, quantity, physical condition, traceability, packaging status, and handling history.

These factors are related but not interchangeable. A high purity result does not independently confirm sequence identity. A correct identity result does not show whether the material was exposed to conditions that could affect a later experiment. Likewise, an intact seal supports confidence in handling, but it is not a substitute for analytical documentation.

The right standard depends on the intended use. A preliminary method-development exercise may rely on a supplier’s product-specific documentation plus basic incoming inspection. A quantitative, comparative, or stability-sensitive workflow may require additional confirmation under the laboratory’s own methods. The key is to define acceptance criteria before reviewing the sample rather than deciding what is acceptable after it arrives.

How to Evaluate Sample Integrity at Receipt

Receiving is the point where a laboratory can preserve the strongest possible chain of documentation. Record the condition of the shipment promptly and avoid making assumptions based on appearance alone.

Begin by matching the product label, lot or batch identifier, quantity, and item description against the purchase record. The lot number should connect directly to the corresponding Certificate of Analysis, not merely to a generic specification sheet. If the documentation cannot be matched to the material in hand, place the sample in quarantine until the discrepancy is resolved.

Then inspect the outer package and primary container for visible concerns. Look for compromised seals, broken or damaged vials, missing labels, moisture exposure, or evidence that the package was not maintained as expected during transit. Document observations with the date, receiver’s initials, and photographs when your quality system calls for them.

A practical receiving review should confirm all of the following:

  • The product name, lot number, and quantity match the order and accompanying records.
  • The container is intact, legible, and free of obvious damage or tampering.
  • The product-specific COA is available and internally consistent with the label.
  • Shipping and storage conditions are reviewed against the supplier’s handling guidance.
  • Any exception is documented before the material is released to inventory.

Do not treat a minor discrepancy as a clerical inconvenience. A mismatched lot number, incomplete record, or unclear container label can break traceability at the moment it matters most. A controlled hold is generally preferable to introducing uncertainty into a research program.

Review the Certificate of Analysis Carefully

A Certificate of Analysis is a core integrity document, but its value depends on specificity and readability. The strongest COAs identify the product and lot, state the test methods or analytical categories used, report the applicable results, and include a release or quality review reference.

For peptide materials, researchers commonly look for identity confirmation and purity analysis. Mass spectrometry may support molecular mass confirmation, while chromatographic methods such as HPLC can provide a reported purity profile. The document should make clear what result applies to the specific lot being evaluated.

Read purity values in context. A stated 99%+ purity standard is meaningful when paired with lot-specific analytical results, but purity is not a universal measure of fitness for every protocol. Depending on the research question, researchers may also need information on salt form, water content, residual solvents, counterions, concentration, or other parameters. Requesting only a single purity figure can leave important questions unanswered.

Also review dates and document versioning. The COA should correspond to the current lot, and the laboratory should retain the version reviewed at receipt. If a document is revised, maintain a record of both the original and updated versions, along with the reason for the change when provided.

Independent third-party testing adds useful separation between manufacturing and verification. It does not eliminate the need for laboratory controls, but it gives procurement and research teams a stronger basis for assessing reported quality. Separate Identity, Purity, and Stability

One of the most common evaluation errors is treating these three concepts as the same question. They are not.

Identity asks whether the material is what the label says it is. Purity asks how much of the sample is the intended analyte relative to detectable impurities under the reported method. Stability asks whether the material remains within acceptable condition over time and under defined handling conditions.

A sample may meet an identity check and show strong initial purity while still being unsuitable after repeated exposure to unfavorable conditions. Conversely, a stable sample may be analytically consistent but not meet the identity requirements of a particular project. Each question requires its own evidence.

For higher-control workflows, establish risk-based confirmation testing. This may include an incoming identity screen, chromatographic comparison to a reference material where appropriate, moisture assessment, or reanalysis after a defined storage interval. The scope should reflect the material’s role in the study, the consequence of an incorrect result, and the capability of the laboratory.

Protect Integrity Through Storage and Handling Controls

A well-documented sample can lose value quickly if its condition after receipt is not controlled. Follow the product-specific storage information provided by the supplier and align it with the laboratory’s written procedures. Record the storage location, receipt date, initial container condition, and any aliquoting or transfer events.

Limit unnecessary handling. Repeated opening, uncontrolled exposure to ambient conditions, and unclear container transfers can create preventable variability. When aliquots are needed, label each container with the original lot number, preparation date, concentration or mass where applicable, preparer’s initials, and storage condition. The goal is to preserve a direct line from each working container back to the original release documentation.

Temperature records matter when the workflow depends on controlled storage. Review excursion records against predefined acceptance criteria rather than relying on a general assumption that a brief event is harmless. The correct disposition may depend on duration, temperature range, container closure, sample format, and whether the material will be used in a sensitive analytical application.

Use Fit-for-Purpose Verification Before Critical Work

Supplier documentation establishes a starting point. It does not replace fit-for-purpose verification in the laboratory. Before assigning a sample to a high-value experiment, ask what failure mode would most affect the result and whether the available evidence addresses it.

For example, if the work depends on accurate mass-based preparation, the laboratory may need to account for material form and moisture-related variability. If the work relies on chromatographic separation, an incoming analytical comparison may be more relevant. If material will remain in inventory for an extended period, a scheduled recheck may be warranted.

This approach prevents both under-testing and unnecessary testing. Not every vial requires the same analytical burden, but every vial should have a documented rationale for release into the intended workflow. A proportionate quality plan preserves resources while protecting data credibility.

Document Decisions, Not Just Results

Integrity evaluation is complete only when another qualified person can understand the decision later. Retain the COA, receiving inspection record, shipment observations, storage history, deviations, and any internal test data in a controlled file or laboratory system.

When a sample fails an acceptance criterion, document the disposition clearly. The material may be quarantined pending clarification, rejected, or restricted to a less sensitive noncritical use if your written procedures permit it. What matters is that the decision is traceable, consistent, and made before questionable material influences research data.

Sample integrity is ultimately a discipline of evidence. Choose suppliers that provide lot-specific documentation, verify what arrived, preserve handling records, and apply analytical checks that match the stakes of the work. That process gives every research team a clearer foundation for results they can defend.
